## Where this period is heading

The eighteenth run put four papers from three categories into one
argument: verification that is sampled decays with artifact size,
verification that is inferred after the fact decays with model turnover,
and verification captured at generation time decays with neither. This
run read a fourth category and the argument did not just hold. It
acquired the admission.

**The field has stopped auditing models and started auditing its own
judges.** In the 100 cs.CV entries read, at least five are audits of the
oversight instrument rather than of a model: a criterion-conditioned
judge benchmark [E176], a formal treatment of what a visual-grounding
probe can and cannot measure [E177], a unified image-evaluation scheme
with grounded explanations, an audit of judge-guided image selection on
culturally situated prompts, and a matched-budget audit of recaptioned
supervision distributions [E175]. Five in a 3.3 per cent sample is a
subfield, not a coincidence.

**And the strongest of those audits concedes Ursa's distinction in its
own limitations section.** VisionQ exists to test whether a judge picks
the preferred output *for the right reason*, which is the exact failure
Ursa Minor's pitch attributes to preference proxies. To get a label for
"right reason" at all, its authors went to the best source available for
open-ended visual judgment, which was 1,409 peer-reviewed CVPR and ICCV
papers, and took the criterion the papers' own authors stated. Their
limitations section then says what that cost them [E176]:

> Dependence on author-stated claims. Ground truth for each question is
> derived from author claims, which may be aspirational, imprecise, or
> biased toward favorable comparisons.

> Claims are not independently verified against human perceptual ground
> truth.

That is the declared-versus-revealed distinction, written by the people
with the strongest incentive not to write it. The best label the field
can currently buy for open-ended work is **what the author said was
best about their own work**. Ursa's artifact label is what the work
kept. Nobody in that paper judged anything wrongly. They reached for
ground truth and found a claim where ground truth was supposed to be.

**The number says how much room that leaves.** Of twenty open- and
closed-source judges evaluated, the strongest reaches 63.1 per cent
accuracy against 32.2 per cent chance, and the paper refuses to call
that good because it never measured a human [E176]. So the instrument
labs use to grade open-ended output is, on the one benchmark built to
test its reasons rather than its answers, roughly twice chance with no
ceiling established.

**What a builder should do differently this week.** Nothing in Ursa's
code changes on this evidence. What changes is the sentence Ursa Minor
uses with a lab, and it gets shorter. Until now this seat has had to
argue that preference proxies grade appearance. It no longer has to
argue it, because a 2026 benchmark built by nine authors to test exactly
that reports a best-in-class score of 0.631 against 0.322 chance and
names its own ground truth as author claims. That is a citation, not a
position. The honest limit on it is stated in finding 1's confidence
note: this is computer vision, and Ursa sells writing, research and
applied engineering. The mechanism transfers and the measurement does
not.

## What is RISING

### 1. The judge-audit subfield, and a best-in-class judge at twice chance on a reason-conditioned task

**High confidence on the numbers, which were read in the full text.
Lower confidence on transfer, stated rather than buried.**

VisionQ [E176] is the first benchmark built from peer-reviewed
comparison figures that grounds every judgment in a named visual
criterion, crediting a judge only when it picks the output the paper's
authors identified as best *on that criterion*. It comprises 1,409
papers, more than 1,800 validated figures, 3,911 hand-annotated data
points, and a six-axis, 51-leaf taxonomy of the criteria behind
qualitative judgment. Twenty judges were evaluated. The strongest
reaches 63.1 per cent against 32.2 per cent chance, and reliability
varies sharply across criteria. The authors also DPO-tune a Gemma-4-E4B
judge on symmetric evidence pairs, which cuts last-option predictions by
7.0 points and raises accuracy by 2.5 points.

Its three stated limitations are all about the label and not the model:
dependence on author-stated claims, no human baseline, and possible
pretraining exposure because the source figures come from public
2023-2024 papers.

**Why this is Ursa's finding and not a curiosity.** `CLAUDE.md` §5 says
Ursa Minor sells the missing verifier for open-ended work. This paper is
an independent attempt to build one for a visual domain, by a team with
no connection to Ursa, and it lands on a declared label because a
revealed one was not available to them. The gap Ursa claims exists is
the gap this paper documents itself falling into.

**Confidence on transfer.** The domain is computer vision figures. Ursa's
span classifications operate over text in writing, research and applied
engineering. No claim is made here that 0.631 is the number for text
judges, because that was not measured and this seat did not measure it.

### 2. The probe that checks whether a judge used its evidence is provably one-sided

**Medium confidence. Abstract page read, full text not read, and no
claim here is made about what the paper does not contain.**

"A Low Grounding Score Is Not an Ungrounded Judge" [E177] takes the
counterfactual probe used to audit visual grounding, which edits the
image so the ground truth flips, holds the reasoning trace fixed and
asks whether the verdict follows. It formalises this as the Verdict
Grounding Score and argues the score cannot be read the way such scores
are read, because a verdict only responds to an edit that reaches the
judge's decision-relevant reading. The error is therefore one-sided: the
score can only make a judge look **less** grounded than it is, so the
practical failure is a false alarm in which an auditor discards a usable
overseer. Auditing nine judges, the predicted ordering held strictly
across the primary pool, and the typical judge acted on only about half
the edits whose attribute it could otherwise resolve. The paper's own
rule is that an image-side counterfactual score should never be reported
alone, because a detection probe on the unedited image upper-bounds it
and costs nothing extra.

**Why it matters here, stated as an open question rather than a finding.**
Ursa's own `generated_deleted` and `no_generation_provenance` labels are
attributions produced by a bounded check over an artifact. Whether this
paper's one-sided-error argument has an analogue in the span resolver is
a real question and this run did not answer it. It is written down so
the next run inherits the question instead of the hunch.

## Corrections to the record

### Correction 1 — an arXiv monthly listing is an announcement window, not a submission window, and the slice this seat reads first is where the difference concentrates

**This is a defect in this seat's primary instrument, measured twice.**

Both papers above were found in the cs.CV listing for October 2026 and
both carry `2610` identifiers. Their submission histories, read as
primary sources rather than inferred, say otherwise [E178], [E179]:

| Found in | Identifier | v1 submission date |
|---|---|---|
| cs.CV listing, October 2026 | arXiv:2610.00111 | Wed, 9 Sep 2026 05:55:25 UTC |
| cs.CV listing, October 2026 | arXiv:2610.00666 | Wed, 30 Sep 2026 20:03:52 UTC |

So the listing month and the identifier prefix both track **announcement**,
and a paper can sit three weeks behind its own identifier. A run that
dates a paper by the listing it was found in, or by its identifier, is
wrong by up to that much.

**The part that makes this systematic rather than trivia.** Delayed
papers receive their identifier when they are announced, which puts them
at the **low end** of the month's identifier range. Entries 1 to 100 are
exactly the slice a sampling run reads first, and both instances here
came from it. So the sampling order this seat uses concentrates the
dating error rather than spreading it.

**What follows.** Nothing in this brief is affected, because this brief
dates both papers from their submission histories. What is affected is
any future sentence of the form "N entries for October 2026" read as
that month's work, and any recency claim resting on an identifier. The
fix is one line of method, which is to read the submission history
before dating a paper, and it is not proposed as a charter edit this run
for the reasons under Step 4.

### Correction 4 — why two charter files are on one branch at all, which is a structure and not a breach

the engineer rider, and later runs proposed this seat's own charter
[E182]. The branch carries both because the charter's own rule orders
this seat to build on its open pull request rather than start a second

**The general shape, which is the part worth the owner's attention.**
This seat's Boundaries clause is written per run, allowing "one file

manufacture an appearance of a breach, and a reviewer acting on the
appearance is behaving correctly.

Filed rather than fixed, for the reasons under Step 4.
