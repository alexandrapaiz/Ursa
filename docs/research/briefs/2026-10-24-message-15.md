## What is RISING

### 1. Revocation is a two-state problem, and the second state is measured for the first time (high confidence, abstract in full, cross-model, six providers)

arXiv:2610.30698, *Governance-State Corruption in Long-Term Memory for
LLM Agents: A Cross-Model Stress Test*, Liu, Wang, Shen and Shang,
submitted 22 October 2026, cs.AI and cs.SE [E1]. Its first sentence is
the finding: "An LLM agent can retrieve a memory correctly yet use it
incorrectly after its validity state changes." The object of study is
what the authors call governance-state corruption, meaning "faults that
alter which retained records enter model-visible context while stored
content and tasks remain fixed."

The measurements, quoted rather than rounded. Across six providers, 731
benchmark-grounded bases yield 2,080 matched fault pairs. In offline
control-plane checks, corruption changes the operative projection in
**96.25%** of pairs, and oracle-backed enforcement restores it in
**100%**. Downstream, clean-minus-corrupted contrasts are **40.54
percentage points** in LongMemEval binary correctness, with a 95% base
cluster interval of [30.32, 50.71], and 30.60 points in rescaled Memora
FAMA, interval [23.31, 37.69]. The authors mark their own full-cohort
frozen-scorer figures of -27.55 and +27.51 points as "descriptive
proxies, not validated native semantic population effects," and that
scoping is theirs and is preserved here because dropping it would
overclaim the paper.

Why this is load-bearing rather than interesting. `CLAUDE.md`
constraint 2 says the user can always see, edit, revoke and delete what
has been inferred about them. This paper says that in a cross-model
memory setting, whether a record is stored and whether it reaches
model-visible context are two different states, that the second is
corruptible at 96.25% in the control plane, and that the swing on the
task is around forty points.

**And the verdict on Ursa's own code is positive, which is worth saying
plainly because this record's habit is to find defects.**
`ursa-major/src/consent.ts` is built fail-closed, keeps every consent
transition in an append-only history, and makes erasure reach the
derived axiom rather than only the record. More to the point, the
property this paper argues should be tested is already tested here. The
enumeration of test names in `ursa-major/src/consent.test.ts`,
`consent.e2e.test.ts` and `disclosure.test.ts` contains "revoking stops
disclosure again", "a forgotten record is excluded from disclosure even
while consent is granted", and "tombstones the axiom instead of
deleting it, so a re-distill cannot resurrect it" [E2]. The middle one
is exactly the cross-state case, where the store says forgotten and
consent says granted and the projection must still exclude.

The honest gap is narrower and is a scoping decision rather than a bug.
`DisclosureScope` in `consent.ts` is a single scope, `'minor-aggregate'`,
and the paper's setting is six providers. So the property is tested
along the state dimension and untested along the vendor dimension,
which is the dimension Ursa Major's whole promise runs along.

### 2. The label this company sells has a measured confound, and the discriminator for it is already written and does not cross the boundary (high confidence, abstract in full plus a first-hand code read)

arXiv:2610.27801, *Looks Fine to Me: Understanding Oversight in Agentic
Interactions*, Dissanayake, Samaradivakara, Lee, Weerasinghe, Saha, Maes
and Nanayakkara, submitted 20 October 2026, cs.HC [E3]. This seat's own
record logs this paper at `docs/research/briefs/2026-10-24.md` line 2421
as "seen and not opened, cited for nothing," so opening it closes a gap
the record itself named.

A formative study at N=12 found that "users accepted plausible outputs
without thorough evaluation, and only recognized these lapses in
retrospect." A controlled within-subject study at N=36 then had
participants do programming tasks with an agent producing correct
outputs, silent defects, or visible failures, instrumented with task
outcomes, interaction logs, gaze, physiological signals and
self-reports. The result: "Participants were often highly confident
while missing silent defects: they performed generic checks, failed to
generate diagnostic evidence, or even misinterpreted defect evidence."

Why this bears on the commercial object. `CLAUDE.md` §1 rests the
artifact's value on the claim that "Nobody judged the output. The work
either used it or didn't." The strongest span class is
`survived_verbatim`, generated and kept unchanged. This paper measures,
with gaze and physiology rather than self-report alone, that a human
keeping a plausible output is sometimes not endorsing it but failing to
detect a silent defect. So `survived_verbatim` conflates "kept because
it was right" with "kept because the defect was silent."

**Ursa already knows this and has already built the answer.**
`ursa-major/src/lifespan.ts` opens by naming the same failure mode in
its own words, that a span the user "kept, shipped, and three commits
later the real work removed" is "today labelled `survived_verbatim` and
sold as a positive. It is a false positive" [E4]. It computes
`decayRate`, the fraction of a record's own survived verdict that the
later real work overturned, and `annotateDurability` is wired into the
CLI at `ursa-major/src/bin/ursa.ts` line 52.

**The gap is that the number stops at the device boundary.** A grep for
`decayRate` across `ursa-major/src` returns `lifespan.ts`, its test, and
`types.ts`, and nothing else [E5]. The disclosed batch in
`ursa-major/src/disclosure.ts` carries `survival_scalar` and
`sample_generations` and no durability field at all. So the scalar a lab
pays against is the boolean-at-`finalSha` survival that `lifespan.ts`
documents as carrying a false positive, and the correction is computed
on-device and never crosses. That is the sharpest engineer item of this
run and it is item 1 below.

### 4. The only published benchmark for cross-model memory drift grades with models on synthetic items, and its own result is null (high confidence, full text read for construction)

arXiv:2610.05124, *Memory Canonicalization: A Framework and Benchmark
for Cross-Model Drift in Persistent LLM Memory*, Vadnere and Lonarkar,
4 October 2026, cs.AI [E6]. It proposes a companion benchmark, CMSC-E,
and this is the item most at risk of being mis-cited, so both halves are
stated.

The construct is real and is the thing Ursa Major has no instrument for.
The problem statement is that "an identical stored memory object,
retrieved by two different LLMs under otherwise identical conditions,
may not be interpreted the same way, factually or emotionally."

The result does not support a strong claim. The improvement in
cross-model emotional consistency is +0.050 with a 95% bootstrap
interval of [0.013, 0.086] and p = 0.010, and the authors state it "does
not survive Bonferroni, Holm, or Benjamini-Hochberg correction across
the six comparisons tested." They add that "None of the factual-drift
(CMSD) comparisons reach significance at any correction level," and
label the whole as "exploratory rather than confirmatory."

The construction, read from the full text rather than the abstract,
decides how much weight the benchmark can carry. CMSD is "pairwise
bidirectional entailment agreement" between claim sets extracted from
each model's response, averaged per memory object. The emotional score
is produced "using two different judges," an emotional-validator
classifier and a factual-validator NLI model. The dataset is "176
synthetic memory objects, stratified across 16 strata," generated from
templates, sent to "three models under test: one closed-weight frontier
model, one closed-weight smaller/faster model, and one open-weight
model" [E9].

So the one published instrument for Ursa Major's central assumption is
model-graded on synthetic objects, and its authors list independent
judge models and human-validated rendering among the work still needed.
That is precisely the position `CLAUDE.md` §1 stakes out against, where
"the label is supplied by the artifact rather than by a grader." The
honest reading is not that the paper is weak but that the territory has
a named construct, a published benchmark, and no outcome-based
instrument in it at all, which is an opening rather than a threat.

## Step 4 — meta-review proposal

One proposal, in `prompts/research-agent.md`, and it is the only file
under `prompts/` this run writes.

**The problem.** The charter asks each run to re-derive the listing set
from its own evidence tables. That instruction is correct and it does
not survive contact with a run, because re-derivation is judgement under
time pressure and the cheapest judgement is to re-read a category this
seat already knows. The evidence is a pattern with two instances and
three failures to execute. cs.IR produced the single most valuable
finding in the record and went unopened for nine briefs, which is the
thirteenth run's own finding 48 and error 32. cs.AI met the clause's
explicit criterion and went unopened for three further runs after the
clause landed, and opening it today produced three papers absent from
the record on Ursa Major's central claim.

**The change.** Keep the re-derivation rule and add the derived set to
the charter as written state, with the categories already opened and the
categories still never opened named explicitly, so a run inherits the
answer and spends its judgement on updating the list rather than on
rebuilding it. The list after this run is: opened, cs.MA, cs.IR, cs.CY,
cs.MM, cs.CL, cs.CR, cs.HC and cs.AI. Never opened and still
qualifying, cs.LG, cs.CV and cs.ET.

**What it is not.** It is a proposal. The owner's merge is the
promotion, and under the standing charter freeze a pull request touching
`prompts/` waits on her regardless of its check. This branch already
carries charter edits inherited from #370 and #366, so it is Tier C
either way and this proposal adds no new review class to it.
