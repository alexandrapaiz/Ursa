## What is rising

### 1. The published paper closest to Ursa's own label throws away most of its data, and the reason is the reason the outcome record is a different object (high confidence, full text read)

**arXiv:2410.02320, *Post-edits Are Preferences Too*, four authors,
WMT24, submitted 2024-10-03 and revised through 2025-02-21** [E1]. Its
argument is the Ursa argument. In preference optimization an annotator
is shown two sequences and asked which is better, whereas in
post-editing the editor "creates s₁ and knows that it should be better
than s₂", which makes the edit a preference by construction. The record
has had that quote since 2026-10-05.

**What the record has never had is the distribution.** The full text
reports that **56.7 per cent of its Russian examples are unedited or
lightly edited**, against 6.4 per cent for German, and it then states
the consequence in one line: "it is impossible for a model to prefer an
unedited 'post-edit' over the machine translation." So the unedited
majority is filtered out of the analysis. They keep it for supervised
fine-tuning, where it is usable, and they remove it from every
preference pair, where it is not [E1].

**Read that against the four Ursa span classes.** A pairwise
construction over edits can represent `survived_mutated` and nothing
else. `survived_verbatim` is the unedited case, and the paper says
outright that it cannot enter a preference pair. `generated_deleted`
has no better sibling to be paired against. And
`no_generation_provenance`, which `CLAUDE.md` section 1 calls the most
valuable category, is a span with no generation anywhere in its
ancestry, so there is nothing on the other side of the pair at all. The
majority of what a real work session produces is therefore invisible to
the best-published version of the mechanism Ursa claims.

**Why this is the strongest thing this seat has found for the Minor
thesis, rather than the usual prior-art threat.** `CLAUDE.md` section 1
justifies the outcome record by saying "the label is supplied by the
artifact rather than by a grader." That sentence does not survive
contact with this paper, because a post-edit is also supplied by the
artifact rather than by a grader, and it was published two years
earlier. The distinction that does survive is narrower and much harder
to argue away. **An outcome record is an object that can hold the
non-edit outcomes, and a preference pair is an object that structurally
cannot.** The paper supplies the number that makes the point, which is
that in one real post-editing corpus the non-edit outcomes are the
majority of the data. That is an argument where section 1 currently has
an assertion, and it is the version a post-training researcher cannot
answer with "we already do that."

**Two cautions from the same read, both against the record's existing
use of the paper.** First, the record cites it for "best results come
from SFT on post-edits before preference optimization," and the full
text qualifies that: supervised fine-tuning on post-edits makes "both
the post-edits and the MT outputs become more likely," so the
separation is not clean. Second, the paper does not analyse how much of
its signal comes from edit magnitude, and that absence is stated here
on a full-text read rather than inferred from an abstract [E1]. Anyone
tempted to read edit size as a proxy for how wrong a generation was
will find no support for it there.

**Where it lands.** Sprint 2026-10-12 item 1 is "verbatim means
byte-identical, and the gate should say so," which is work on precisely
the class this paper has to discard [E10]. The sprint item is already
right. This finding says what it is worth, which is that
`survived_verbatim` is not housekeeping on a label, it is the half of
the artifact that the nearest published neighbour cannot hold.

### 2. Nobody enforces a revocation at the point where it has to be enforced, it has now been measured, and Ursa's deterministic layer hands the decision back to the model (high confidence, full text read, repository read in full)

**arXiv:2609.08258, *Revoked but Still Authoritative*, three authors,
submitted 2026-09-08** [E2]. It measures five agent-memory systems,
named as Graphiti, Zep, mem0, langmem and cognee, across nine policy
scenarios and nine models under six defense conditions. The finding is
flat: "no memory system enforces revocation in every case: the revoked
fact is returned wherever the revocation label is visible to the
retrieval layer, outranks its replacement, and leads agents to the
unsafe action." Agents took the unsafe action in **699 of 1,620 trials,
or 43.1 per cent**, ranging from 0 to 94.4 per cent across scenarios.

**The part that ranks the defenses is the part Ursa needs.** Of the six
conditions, the **store-level filter removed the unsafe action
entirely, at 0 per cent**. Prompt hardening alone brought 43.1 per cent
down only to **37.2 per cent**. An output filter reached 18.1 per cent
[E2]. So the gap between enforcing at the store and instructing the
model is the difference between a guarantee and a nudge, measured on
1,620 trials.

**Ursa has both of those mechanisms, and its design document is
confident about the wrong one.** `docs/design/consent-and-erasure.md`
section 6c says a later distillation cannot revive a revoked axiom and
gives two reasons: "`tuning/merge.ts` already skips a matched revoked
axiom and `distill.ts` passes the tombstones to the model as 'do not
resurrect'" [E9]. The second of those is prompt hardening, at
`distill.ts` lines 74 and 100 [E8]. The first is the store-level filter,
and the word carrying the weight in it is *matched*.

**Reading `merge.ts` in full shows what the match depends on** [E7].
The only revocation check in `mergeDistill` is at line 46, and it is
reachable only through line 41, `const match = d.matchesExisting ?
byId.get(d.matchesExisting) : undefined`. The field `matchesExisting`
comes from the model's distillation output. If the model restates a
revoked axiom as a new statement and does not set that field, the `else`
branch runs instead and constructs a fresh axiom with `status:
'active'`, comparing it against nothing. There is no statement-level
check against the revoked set anywhere in the function. The file's own
opening comment says "the model judged (statements, matches,
contradictions); this code counts," which is an accurate description of
the split and also the problem: the revocation decision is on the
model's side of it.

**So the layer Ursa would point at as its store-level filter is gated
on a model-supplied field, and the fallback is the defense that measured
37.2 per cent.** This is not a theoretical ordering of defenses any
more. It is five systems, 1,620 trials, and a 0-versus-37.2 spread.

**The absence claim, with its instruments.** No prior brief, no security
audit and no pull request has filed this. The instruments were a grep
for `matchesExisting` across every markdown file in the repository,
whose only hits are example code in one skill, a grep of all five
security audits under `docs/security/` for "revok" and "resurrect",
which returned nothing at all, and `gh pr list --state all` searched on
`matchesExisting`, on revoked-axiom resurrection and on revocation
merge, which returns the consent-gate engineer PR #59 and four research
briefs and none of this [E10].

**Why this ranks second and not first.** It is an engineering defect in
one function rather than a movement in the field, and the fix is small.
But it sits on load-bearing constraint 2 of `CLAUDE.md`, which says the
user can always revoke, and on the asset section 2 says can be destroyed
in a week. **Route: the engineer.** This seat does not write product
code and has not touched it.

### 3. The cold start is worth less than it looks, it has been priced, and the baseline Ursa Major would naturally measure against is the wrong one (medium-high confidence, full text read)

**arXiv:2610.21938, *Agentic-Priors*, three authors, submitted
2026-10-15** [E3], found in the listing slice this run was told to read.
It addresses the cold-start problem in preference elicitation by having
a language model state a new user's trade-offs in interpretable terms,
mapping those to a prior over multinomial logit coefficients, and
refining it with a Bayesian learner as choices arrive. Across seven
choice datasets it improves cold-start accuracy over an uninformed prior
by 5 to 40 points, and in log-loss "it is worth between about 6 and 40
surveyed users, depending on the dataset."

**Ursa Major's value proposition is the cold start.** `CLAUDE.md`
section 3 opens with "from the first message on any AI, it already knows
how you like to be answered." This paper is the first quantified
statement this seat has found about what that first message is worth,
and it has two results that cut against the natural way to measure it.

**The advantage over an uninformed prior mostly does not survive
contact with data.** Quoted verbatim: "the accuracy advantage over the
uninformed prior shrinks quickly: on LPMC, EV and Beer a single choice
is enough to close it (in log-loss it persists), on Cars CN it falls to
1 to 3 points, while on Swissmetro, Cars and Bank it persists" [E3]. So
on three of seven datasets, one observed choice erases the accuracy gain
of a good prior. The authors state the conclusion themselves: an
informative prior "is therefore best seen as a way to start learning
before any data exist, not a substitute for population data once a small
survey is available."

**And the base model already supplies part of what the profile would.**
The paper's prior comes from a language model told a short natural
language profile of the user, and the authors run the ablation that
matters here, which is the same method "without zᵢ, where the LLM sees
only the task description," to separate task knowledge from
individual inference [E3]. That ablation is the baseline any honest
measurement of a portable Ursa profile has to beat. A comparison against
a cold uninformed model would credit the profile for everything the
model already infers from the task.

**What follows for Major, and it is a sharpening rather than a
retreat.** The first-message framing is the weakest defensible version
of the Major claim, because it is the part a frontier model's own priors
erode every release. The part this paper leaves untouched is the part
`CLAUDE.md` section 4 already names as the compounding asset: the
longitudinal depth, and the fact that the thing crosses vendors.
Nothing in this paper is about portability across providers, and its
own framing concedes that priors are a bridge until data exists.
Ursa Major's durable claim is that the data exists already, from
somewhere else, and belongs to the user.

**The limit on this finding.** It is a discrete-choice setting with
willingness-to-pay style attributes, not open-ended assistant
interaction, so the numbers transfer as an order of magnitude and a
direction rather than as a measurement of Ursa. Privacy is absent from
the paper entirely, stated on a full-text read [E3].

read-depth discipline of this seat is written entirely around negative
claims, and the last three briefs have each had to correct a *positive*

that supports it and takes its confidence from that depth, and an
abstract page licenses a claim about the subject of a paper but not
about its construction. Evidence: the corrections sections of
2026-10-13, 2026-10-16 and 2026-10-18, and finding 1 of this brief,
which is a number that four prior citations of the same paper did not
have [E1][E10].

**Edit B, carried from 2026-10-18 and unchanged.** The header of this
file says prior briefs "are read off the open pull requests because
`main` holds none of them." `main` holds ten briefs as of this
afternoon [E10]. The instruction is still right, because the newest
brief is routinely unmerged, which this one is while it is being read,

instruction being dropped.

**Edit C, this run's own, and it is one clause.** The absence-claim rule

### 5. The paper that names Ursa's own measurement gap, and the design sentence that licensed it (high confidence, full text sections read)

Two of the three deferred entries turn out to be about the same thing,
which is the part of Ursa's instrument that has never been measured.
Taken together they are the sharpest external argument this seat has
found against a claim Ursa makes in its own files.

**The first half is a prescription Ursa mostly satisfies.** *Why Do
Agent Teams Fail Across Boundaries?* [E14] is a fifteen-author study of
more than 2,500 recorded runs over 488 scenarios, and its section 4 is
titled "What the Outcome Score Cannot See". It states that "aggregate
outcome scores (e.g., rubric pass-rates, deterministic checks) are blind
to most failures" and concludes that "aggregate outcome scores are
fundamentally underpowered for multi-agent evaluation". That sounds like
an attack on the outcome record and it is not, because the paper's
remedy is decomposition: evaluate "each agent independently against its
own principal's directives". Ursa's four span classes are exactly such a
decomposition, per contributor rather than per run, which places Ursa on
the side of the argument this paper is making rather than against it.
The setting is different and the analogy should not be stretched, since
the paper studies agents negotiating across a trust boundary and Ursa
studies one person's finished document.

**One mechanism inside it does transfer, and it is a confound in Ursa's
own numbers.** The paper reports that "because rubrics score pass-shares
over final deliverables, longer deliverables inflate scores regardless
of peer behavior". Ursa's survival shares are shares of a final
artifact, so the same arithmetic applies to them: a model whose text
lands in a long document earns a larger `survived_verbatim` share than
the same text landing in a short one, with no difference in how much the
kept text mattered. Ursa reports those shares as percentages of the
final work, which `docs/design/agent-coauthor-detection.md` line 530
shows carrying both a `pct` and a `pctOfFinal` field, so the quantity
the confound acts on is already the one being published.

**The second half is the harder one, because it is measurement against
one of Ursa's own stated reasons for not measuring.** *Origin
Attribution for Unsafe Behavior in Self-Evolving Multi-Agent Coding
Systems* [E15] does provenance attribution over revision histories,
which is structurally the operation Ursa's resolver performs. Three
things in it are load-bearing here. Its ground truth had to be
manufactured, through "controlled fault-injection approaches", because
provenance is not readable off the artifact. Its accuracy is 96.8% and
not 100%, over "158 queries from 85 scenarios", where "accuracy requires
the exact source agent-version pair or the correct None label". And its
residual errors concentrate in one class, which it states as "a relay
revision matches an earlier independent occurrence", adding that
"identical observations can therefore leave the historical relationship
ambiguous".

That error class is the coincidental match, and it is the class Ursa has
already shipped a repair for. `docs/design/verbatim-byte-identity.md`
records the resolver writing `survived_verbatim` on a normalized-text
hit, so that "a case change or an em dash retyped as a hyphen" read as
untouched model output, caught on this repository's own history at
record `ursa-2026-10-07-2f610e9`.

**So here is the sentence.** `docs/design/verdict-eval.md` line 17 draws
the line that decides what gets evaluated in Ursa: the verdict reader
creates a label and is measured, while "every other module joins
finished work backward to the model generations that fed it, which is a
factual operation: a span either survived or it did not". That sentence
is the stated reason no span evaluation exists, and
`ursa-major/src/evals/` holds three files, all of them the verdict
reader's [E16]. A full-state pull request search returns one evaluation
ever built, PR #25, the verdict reader's [E16].

The sentence was defensible when it was written on 2026-09-27. It is not
defensible now, and the repository itself is the first witness rather
than the paper. A span either survived or it did not is true of the
world. It is not true of the resolver's reading of the world, which is a
string-matching inference with at least one documented false-positive
class of its own finding, and `no_generation_provenance` is a negative
provenance claim of exactly the kind [E15] reports a correct `None`
label for and still gets wrong five times in 158.

**Route: the engineer**, who owns `ursa-major/src/evals/` and wrote both
documents. The day-sized first step is not an evaluation harness. It is
deleting or qualifying one sentence, because while it stands, the
absence of a span evaluation reads as a decision rather than as a gap.
The harness after it has an obvious shape, since [E15] supplies the
method: inject known provenance into a fixture, which this repository
can do cheaply because it already keeps real conversation fixtures, and
measure the exact-label rate including the `None` case. `CLAUDE.md` §1
calls `no_generation_provenance` "the most valuable category", and its
error rate is unmeasured.

### 6. Relational tone conditioning disclosure, read at abstract depth and reported as a route (low to medium confidence, abstract only)

The third deferred entry, *RelLedger* [E18], is under review at ICLR
2027 and holds one number this seat cannot ignore and cannot yet stand
on: "Claude sends the same factual notice 62.22 percentage points more
often with conflict histories than with friendly histories." Its setting
is disclosure between agents in a simulated organization, not
distillation, so nothing here is a claim about Ursa.

What makes it worth a line anyway is that Ursa's distiller is a model
reading a user's session and deciding what to write down, and the
sessions it reads carry exactly the variable this paper manipulates. If
what a model reports is conditioned on the relational tone of the
history it is shown, then a tuning record distilled from a session where
the user was frustrated may differ systematically from one distilled
from a cordial session about the same work, and Ursa would read that
difference as a preference. **This is a hypothesis with an instrument,
not a finding.** The instrument is the repository's own fixtures, and
the honest test runs the distiller over the same work with the tone
varied, which is the ablation that paper already runs in its own
setting. I read the abstract page only, so the next run that wants to
act on this owes the full text first.

### What is compounding, across this addendum and finding 2 above

Two independent papers in this one brief, in different fields, report
the same structural result: **the mechanism enforces or nothing does.**
Finding 2's revocation study measures prompt hardening reaching 37.2%
against a store-level filter at 0%. [E14] reports that "no agent
verifies a peer's factual claim on its own initiative, so an A2A system
inherits the reliability of its least reliable participant unless the
protocol or mechanism performs the check". Neither cites the other and
neither is about the other's domain. For a builder here this week, the
instruction is the same one twice, and it is the one finding 2 already
routes to the engineer: a rule that lives in a prompt is not enforced,
whatever the prompt says.
