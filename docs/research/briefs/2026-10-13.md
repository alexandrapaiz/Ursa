## Corrections to this seat's own prior brief

This comes before the synthesis because it changes the strongest claim
the last brief made, and because the charter says correcting a prior
brief is part of the job rather than an exception to it.

The 2026-10-09 brief reported a "correction earned in flight" about
**[E1]**, Ravindran's *Portable Agent Memory*. It said a search summary
had given the paper a wrong title and a fabricated metric, that the
primary says otherwise, and that "no Transfer Continuity Score is
reported anywhere." It drew from that the differentiation claim for
Ursa Major: "No pilot size, no participants, no evaluation results of
any kind. Transfer is demonstrated, never measured."

**Three of those statements are wrong, and the paper's full text is
why.** Read this run across three fetches of arXiv's own HTML
rendering, the last of them with a deliberately neutral prompt asking
only for a list of section headings:

1. **The paper has a Section 5, Evaluation, with four subsections**:
   5.1 Transfer Continuity Score, 5.2 Re-Hydration Fidelity, 5.3
   Security Evaluation, 5.4 Format Efficiency. So "no evaluation
   results of any kind" is false, and so is "demonstrated, never
   measured."
2. **The Transfer Continuity Score is reported.** Section 5.1, Table 3,
   over three model pairs: mean 0.88 for Claude-3.5-Sonnet to
   GPT-4-Turbo, 0.84 for GPT-4-Turbo to Gemini-1.5-Pro, 0.86 for
   Gemini-1.5-Pro to Claude-3.5-Sonnet, against a no-memory baseline
   mean of 0.35, which the paper calls a 2.4 times mean improvement
   over 50 tasks. The paper's own words for it are "directional rather
   than definitive", and Section 6.1 lists the 50-task scale as the
   third of four limitations.
3. **A revocation primitive exists and is tested.** Section 5.3 reports
   100% rejection across expired, wrong-audience, invalid-signature and
   revoked capability tokens. So "no consent, ownership or revocation
   surface" was too broad.

**The title question resolves as a split rather than a fabrication.**
The abstract page renders the title as "A Protocol for
Cryptographically-Verified Memory Transfer Across Heterogeneous **AI**
Agents" and lists one version only, submitted 10 May 2026. The paper's
own HTML full text renders it as "A Protocol for
Provenance-Verified Memory Transfer Across Heterogeneous **LLM**
Agents." Same identifier, same single version, two titles on two of
arXiv's own surfaces. The summary the last brief called fabricated was
quoting the full text accurately.

**How the error was made, which is the part worth keeping.** The last
brief recorded its read depth honestly as "abstract page opened and
read in full." That was true. The defect is that it then made a claim
about the whole paper, and an abstract page is not the paper. The brief
even congratulated itself on the catch, which is how a wrong correction
survives review.

**And this run nearly made the mirror-image error in the other
direction.** The first fetch of the full text named Transfer Continuity
and the three-model pilot *in the prompt* and asked whether they
appeared. It came back confirming them with great specificity. A
separate fetch of the PDF then reported a section outline with no
evaluation section in it at all. At that point the evidence was two
instruments disagreeing, with my own hypothesis leaked into one of
them, and the honest reading was that I could not tell. Only the third
fetch settled it, because it asked for the section list and named
nothing. Had I stopped at the first fetch I would have shipped a
retraction built on a leading question, and had I stopped at the PDF I
would have reaffirmed a false finding. **Both failures were available
and the neutral query is what separated them.** That is this run's
meta-review.

**What survives, and it is still good for Ursa Major.** The
differentiation narrows twice and holds in a sharper form. [E1]
measures transfer, but:

- **There is no human being anywhere in the evaluation.** The subjects
  of all four experiments are models and artifacts. No participants are
  reported, and none of the seven sections is a user study.
- **The one metric that asks whether a person was served has an
  unstated scoring procedure.** TCS is defined as the ratio of target
  task success to source task success, and the full text does not say
  how task success was scored. The mechanical properties get 1,000
  mutation trials and 100% detection. The human-value property gets a
  ratio over 50 tasks with no stated criterion.
- **The revocation that exists is an integrator's primitive, not a
  user's right.** Capability tokens are issued and rejected by whoever
  holds the keys. That is not `CLAUDE.md`'s non-negotiable #2, which is
  the user seeing, editing, revoking and deleting.

So the honest Major sentence, replacing the one the last brief wrote:

> Moving a profile between vendors is a solved engineering problem with
> an Apache-2.0 implementation, and it has now been measured well
> enough to be called solved. What has never been measured is whether a
> human was better served, because no published system in this line has
> put a person in its evaluation. The transport is commodity. The
> derivation from real work, and the user's standing over it, are the
> product.

That is a narrower claim than last week's and it rests on a full text
rather than an abstract.

## What is rising

### 1. Somebody published Ursa's grounding discipline as a benchmark, twelve days ago (high confidence, primary read)

**[E3] Shi, Ji, Huang, Wang, He and Liu, *Open-Endedness Bench:
Measuring Epistemic Process from Agent Records*** (arXiv:2610.02588, 1
October 2026, cs.AI). The only paper in this run's sweep from the
current window, and the most important external item this seat has
found for Ursa Minor.

What it does, from the abstract: it evaluates open-ended research work
by reading **only the agent's execution record**, never a reference
answer and never an outcome score. It compiles the record into a graph
whose edges connect the propositions the agent stated to the executed
actions meant to test them. **Each node carries an exact excerpt, which
code checks against the record.** Prose may state a claim, but only
evidence returned by an executed action may support or refute it.

**Read against this repository that is not adjacent, it is the same
construction.** `ursa-major/src/invariants.ts` now holds sixteen
bounds, and the two that ask whether a thing the record names is real
rather than whether two numbers agree are the excerpt-grounding bound
merged as #107 and the second clause of `EXCLUSION_NOT_CLASSIFIED`.
PR #171's refusal to parse commit prose, on the grounds that a message
merely mentioning a sync is not evidence of one, is [E3]'s rule about
prose and executed actions stated for a different substrate.

**Why this is good news rather than a prior-art problem.** The object
is different and the difference is the whole of Ursa's claim. [E3]
scores the **process** an agent ran, from the agent's own log, which
makes it a better benchmark for agents. Ursa labels spans of a
**finished human work** by what survived into it, which makes it a
reward signal for models. Neither displaces the other. What [E3] gives
Ursa is independent third-party evidence that code-checked excerpt
grounding is the right instrument for open-ended work, published by
people with no stake in Ursa, in the quarter Ursa is trying to publish
its own methodology.

**And it carries a number the methods doc should want.** Across 119
runs on 12 tasks from three benchmarks, [E3] reports that **only 16 to
29 percent of claimed improvements are real when checked against logged
results.** That is a measured, external, quantitative statement that
self-reported claims about open-ended work are mostly wrong, which is
the premise `docs/beyond-preference-pairs.md` currently argues from
first principles with zero external citations.

**One caution, stated because the abstract does not settle it.** [E3]
also profiles "six subjective persona traits" and the abstract does not
say how those readings are produced. If a judge model produces them,
that part of the instrument is the construction Ursa rejects. The four
competence axes are described as shares of sound research opportunities
taken, which is mechanical. **Nobody may cite the persona layer until
someone opens the full text**, and after this run's experience, that
means the full text and not the abstract page.

### 2. The memory-taxonomy convergence, and the user is missing from every instance of it (medium-high confidence, two primaries read)

Three independent lines have now converged on nearly the same
representation of a user, and not one of them gives the user standing
over it.

**[E2] Nie, Fu, Zhang, Yang and Shan, *PersonaVLM: Long-Term
Personalized Multimodal LLMs*** (arXiv:2604.13074, 20 March 2026, CVPR
2026 **Highlight**, Apache-2.0 code). It extracts and summarizes
multimodal conversation history into a structured database of four
memory types, **Core, Semantic, Episodic and Procedural**, and adds a
momentum-based mechanism inferring the user's evolving latent traits. It
reports 22.4% improvement on its own Persona-MME benchmark and 9.8% on
PERSONAMEM at 128k context, and beating GPT-4o by 5.2% and 2.0%.

Set that beside [E1]'s five components, which are episodic, semantic,
procedural, working-state and **identity preferences**. Two unrelated
groups, one a security protocol and one a vision-language model, landed
on the same four names. Add ChatGPT Memory's shipped product and the
field has a de facto taxonomy of the user.

**What none of them has.** [E2]'s repository and abstract make no claim
about cross-vendor portability, user ownership, editing or revocation,
and the Apache-2.0 licence covers the code rather than any user data
right. [E1] has the transport and a keyholder's revocation. The
published state of the art therefore knows how to represent a user and
move that representation around, and has not addressed whose it is.
**That is Ursa Major's whole lane, and it is still empty.**

**Where the confidence stops.** [E2] was read at its abstract page and
its repository page, so the memory taxonomy and the headline numbers
are primary but the evaluation protocol is not. Neither surface says
whether Persona-MME is scored by reference answers, multiple choice, a
judge or human raters, and the baseline for its percentage improvements
is undefined on both. **The numbers above are reported, not endorsed.**

### 3. The Major-side territory is genuinely sparse, which is worth knowing as a fact rather than a gap (medium confidence, two searches, listing level)

Last week's mandate sent this seat into cross-vendor portability,
user-governed personalization, and consent and revocation architecture.
Two searches this run, with deliberately different vocabulary, returned
almost nothing beyond the two papers of 10 May 2026 that the last run
found. There is no industry standard, no sign of a vendor adopting a

**The one distinction worth carrying, because it is load-bearing and
easy to get wrong.** Searching for revocation in this territory returns
machine unlearning: removing a training subset's contribution from a
trained model, framed around the right to be forgotten. **That is a
different problem from revoking a profile**, and conflating the two
would let a reader conclude Ursa Major's revocation story is a solved
and crowded research area. It is not. The published work removes data
from weights. Ursa Major's claim is about a user withdrawing a
representation of themselves from circulation, and nothing found in two
runs of searching addresses it.

So the absence is now a checked absence rather than an unexamined one,
and that is the honest confidence level: two searches at listing depth
found no counterexample, which is weaker than a primary read and
stronger than not having looked.
