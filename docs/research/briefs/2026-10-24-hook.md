## What is rising

### 46. A dense reward for a non-verifiable domain was manufactured out of about one hundred expert judgments, which prices the thing `CLAUDE.md` says labs do not have (high confidence, full text in two passes)

**The paper.** *Expert-Guided Rubrics as Dense Reward Signals for
Reinforcement Learning in Non-Verifiable Domains*, Bansal, Amer,
Guliani, Edke, Matheson and Teran, arXiv 2610.26932, eleven pages, four
figures, for the RL4LLM-Agents workshop at ICAIF [E124]. Nothing in this
repository mentions it. A grep of `docs/`, `prompts/` and `skills/` on
this branch returns nothing, and `gh pr list --state all --search`
returns nothing for the title or for "rubrics dense reward", so this is
unreported here rather than re-filed [E120].

**What it says the problem is, and it is this company's own sentence.**
From the abstract, verbatim: "Retrieval in non-verifiable domains
affords no such oracle: judging whether a query rewrite is 'good'
requires judgment that only a subject-matter expert (SME) can supply
even if this judgment is scarce, and that judgment is graded, not
binary" [E124]. That is the premise Ursa Minor sells against, written
by somebody else, in a paper that then goes on to dissolve it.

**What they did about it, and the number is the finding.** They did not
buy a preference signal and they did not stand up a grading operation.
They took roughly one hundred graded pairs and compiled them into a
judge prompt. The paper's own sentences: "SMEs grade a small sample of
rewrite behavior: roughly 100 (question, rewrite) pairs sampled
(stratified) so the sample spans rewrites that alter vs. copy the
question and that hit vs. miss the answer", and "Those explanations are
encoded as an LLM-executable rubric - a judge prompt with the four
named, individually weighted criteria" [E124]. The reward itself is
"R=a Rretrieval+(1−a) RSME", where "RSME is SME judgment distilled into
a weighted rubric and executed by a frozen judge LLM" [E124]. Reported
result is up to 6% in Recall@3 and 6.7% in MRR@3 over sparse-reward
baselines [E124].

**They ran the check this record keeps asking other people to run.**
Section 5.2 is titled "Do the gains transfer, or did we game our own
referee?", and the answer is a re-scoring by two judges that never saw
the training rubric, with answer quality rising from 0.7432 to about
0.7551 at k=3 and from 0.7528 to 0.7616 at k=10, both cross-family
judges agreeing on the ordering [E125]. Appendix F adds that the rubric
was validated against held-out expert judgments before training, and
that periodic re-grading is the stated guard against drift [E125].

**What it costs this company, stated plainly.** Error 30 of this
morning already said two sentences of `CLAUDE.md` are falsifiable by
citation, on the strength of FDE-Bench and of the Hy-Memory reward
artifact [E126]. This is the third instrument and it is the cheapest
one. A lab buyer who has read it can say that a dense reward for an
open-ended domain costs about one hundred expert judgments plus a frozen
judge, which is a line item rather than an impossibility, and the "no
verifier" half of the pitch does not survive the sentence.

**What it leaves standing, and this is the part worth carrying into a
sales conversation.** The paper's reward is distilled human judgment of
how good a rewrite looks, executed by a model. Its own limitations
section names the failure that follows: "A residual risk is that a
rubric well-specified on the annotated sample drifts on the broader
population", and "Any bias or blind spot in those judgments can
propagate into the trained rewriter and affect which documents users
see" [E124]. A rubric is a population-level proxy with a drift clock on
it, and the guard the paper proposes is to pay the expert again. The
outcome record has no rubric, so it has nothing to drift, and the
replacement sentence for `CLAUDE.md` §5 follows from that rather than
from scarcity: Ursa Minor sells the verifier that does not need a rubric
and therefore does not need re-grading as the population moves. That is
narrower than the sentence on the file today and it is defensible
against this paper.

### 47. The aggregate is the thing that does not transfer, measured across five deployed endpoints, and the aggregate is what Ursa Minor sells (medium to high confidence, abstract verbatim with the section list and the transfer numbers)

**The paper.** *System Attribution in LLM Brand Recommendations: Single
Responses Identify the System, Aggregated Brand Profiles Do Not
Transfer*, Dmitrij Żatuchin, arXiv 2610.00253, thirty pages, five
figures, nine tables, primary cs.IR with cross-lists to cs.CL and
cs.LG [E127]. Nothing in this repository mentions it [E120].

**The corpus.** 6,475 stored responses, 6,324 analysable, collected
between December 2025 and February 2026 from five deployed endpoints
across gift-recommendation, corporate-reputation and category-ownership
queries [E127]. This is stored real traffic against shipped endpoints
rather than a benchmark, which is the same kind of object Ursa Minor
proposes to sell.

**The two results, and the second one is the finding.** At the level of
a single response, the system is trivially identifiable: a character
n-gram classifier reaches 97.84% accuracy across five endpoints against
a 31.5% majority class, 24 formatting statistics alone reach 95.79%, and
masking brand names and capitalised tokens still leaves 97.72% [E127].
At the level of the aggregate, it falls apart across domains. The
abstract, verbatim: "Across domains the aggregate profile fails: a
forest trained on category-ownership units assigns all 22 gift units to
the wrong system, consistent with a reversal in brand volume (8.41
against 0.94 brands per response in gifts, 3.01 against 3.91 in category
ownership), while single responses transfer at 89.92% balanced accuracy"
[E127]. The full text adds that the misassignment carried "a mean stated
confidence of 0.762", and that moved between the two domains the
aggregate classifier "gets 4 of 32 units right" [E128].

**Why this lands on Ursa Minor rather than on an ad-tech audit.** The
product sold is an aggregate of cross-model behaviour, and property one
of three in `CLAUDE.md` §4 is cross-model comparison, meaning what the
same user preferred between one lab's model and a competitor's. This
paper is the first measurement in this record of what happens to such an
aggregate when the task mix changes, and the answer is that it inverts
with high stated confidence. Property three, natural task distribution,
is the thing that makes Ursa's task mix drift by design, because real
users change what they are doing. So the two properties interact in a
direction the pitch does not currently account for: an aggregate
computed over last quarter's task mix is not evidence about this
quarter's, and the confidence attached to it will not warn the buyer.

**The honest bound on this finding.** The paper's own limitations list
names an uncrossed design, so systems do not appear in all domains, a
single collection window, truncation in three subsets, fifty aggregate
units, and brand detection at precision 0.827 with recall at or below
0.43 [E128]. The abstract ends by saying the design "cannot separate the
system from the domain or the harness" [E127]. So this is a measured
warning rather than a proof, and it is filed at medium to high
confidence for that reason. The warning is still the sharpest one
available, because nobody else in this record has measured the object at
all.

**The harness half, which is the part with a file attached.** Two
numbers in the abstract are about the collection apparatus rather than
about the models: 83.1% of one endpoint's answers in one domain end
mid-sentence under a 1,024-token output cap, and in a retrieval-grounded
arm that changes the harness, no Grok answer is attributed to Grok, 0 of
120 [E127]. The harness moved the attribution to zero. That is the
engineer note below.

## Top extraction targets for the skill agent

**One target, ranked first and the only one.**

**1. Deciding whether to manufacture a verifier before committing to
collect outcomes for a quality.** The judgment is: given a quality you
want to improve and no oracle for it, ask first whether a usable grader
can be compiled out of something you already hold, and only collect
outcome evidence when it cannot. The two published instances in this
record reach the same move by different routes. Hy-Memory compiled a
verifier out of 478,000 model-written probes with no human in the reward
path [E126]. The rubric paper compiled one out of about one hundred
expert judgments and a frozen judge LLM [E124]. Both then ran the same
guard against their own construction, which is the procedural half a
skill can carry: Hy-Memory used trap probes, and the rubric paper
re-scored with two judges that had never seen the rubric and reported
the disagreement rate [E125].

**The read-depth caveat, which the skill agent should weigh before
accepting the ranking.** This run read the rubric paper itself. It did
not read Hy-Memory, and the quotations above are taken from the
eleventh run's brief on this branch rather than from the paper, so one
of the two mutually reinforcing sources rests on another run's read.
That is still two published systems with quoted self-check designs
rather than one, which is why this is proposed at all, and the skill
agent is free to reject the ranking.

**Why it is worth a skill rather than a brief entry.** Ursa's own
product is the expensive path in that decision, and no file in this
repository states the cheap path or the test for when it applies.
Nothing in `skills/` covers it: the closest is
`skills/adjudicating-uncertain-spans/`, which is about a span already
collected, and the two open skill pull requests, #348 and #359, are
about the library's own edges rather than about verifier construction
[E120].

## What the engineer should know before building

**`SourcePointer` names the model and not the harness, and finding 47
measured what that costs.** `ursa-major/src/types.ts:102` defines the
pointer as `conversationId`, `model`, `turnIndex`, `generationIndex` and
the character offsets [E132]. Nothing in it records the client, the
surface, or the output cap in force when the generation was produced,
and a grep for `harness`, `maxTokens` and `truncat` over that file
returns nothing relevant [E132].

That is fine while every generation on the `ursa run` path comes through
one client. It stops being fine at the moment the record covers two, and
property one of the Ursa Minor pitch is a comparison across vendors. The
paper measured a case where changing the harness drove attribution of
one system's answers to zero of 120, and a case where 83.1% of one
endpoint's answers in one domain ended mid-sentence under a 1,024-token
cap [E127]. A record that names the model but not the apparatus cannot
tell a buyer whether a difference between two vendors is the model or
the cap, and the buyer is the party most able to notice.

This is a schema note and not a defect in shipped behaviour, so it is
filed for whoever next opens `types.ts` rather than raised as a bug. The
cheapest version is one optional field on `SourcePointer` recording the
client and its output limit, added before the second client exists
rather than after, because a field added later is null for everything
collected before it.
