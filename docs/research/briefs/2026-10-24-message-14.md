## Finding 52: the constant that decides whether the span classifier even looks is absolute, so the classifier goes blind in proportion to the corpus, and the category it silently inflates is the one the product sells as most valuable (high confidence on the mechanism, measured with a named bound on the size)

**This is not the fall-through that finding 41 already filed, and the
difference is the whole point.** Finding 41, in
`docs/research/briefs/2026-10-24-message-2.md`, established that a span
the fuzzy pass never surfaces a candidate for falls through to a bare
`no_generation_provenance` with no `uncertain` flag and no candidate
attached, and that the organization already knew, because the Limits
section of `skills/adjudicating-uncertain-spans/SKILL.md` says so in its
own words. Both halves of that stand and neither is refiled here.

What was not named is **why** a span never surfaces a candidate, and the
answer is one line:

```
ursa-major/src/match.ts:12
/** tokens appearing in more than this many generation segments are too common to seed candidates */
export const MAX_TOKEN_DF = 500
```

`resolve.ts:399` reads that constant inside the fuzzy pass and skips any
token whose bucket is longer than it [E157]. A span every one of whose
distinct tokens is over the cutoff therefore seeds **zero** candidates,
`best` stays null, neither threshold branch can fire, and `resolve.ts`
pushes the bare label at line 465. The matcher did not score that span
low. It never looked at it.

`MAX_TOKEN_DF` appears in exactly two files in this repository, both of
them source, and in no document, design note, skill or prior brief
[E158]. So the constant that decides which spans the classifier is
capable of seeing has never been written about.

**Why it is a defect and not a tuning choice.** The threshold is an
absolute count on a quantity that grows with the corpus. Document
frequency is measured over generation segments, and the number of
generation segments grows every time a user runs the tool. So the set of
tokens the matcher refuses to seed on grows monotonically with the
dataset, and the set of spans the matcher cannot see grows with it. A
threshold expressed as a fraction of corpus size would hold recall
roughly constant as the corpus grew. This one trades recall away for
depth, which is the opposite of the premise in `CLAUDE.md` §2 that the
longitudinal depth of the dataset is an asset that compounds daily.

**Measured, with the bound attached.** This run measured the blind
fraction against corpus size, using this repository's own markdown as a
proxy generation corpus and reimplementing the product's own
`normalize`, `tokens` and `segmentProse` rules so the predicate under
test is the shipped one [E159]. Segments were shuffled under a fixed
seed before prefixes were taken, so each row is a representative sample
rather than an alphabetical one. "Substantive" means a segment at or
above `MIN_VERBATIM_LEN` normalized characters with at least four
distinct tokens, which excludes the one-word fragments that segmentation
produces at document boundaries and that would otherwise inflate every
row.

| generation segments | tokens over the cutoff | substantive spans that seed zero candidates |
|---|---|---|
| 1,000 | 0 | 0.00 per cent |
| 2,500 | 1 | 0.00 per cent |
| 10,000 | 16 | 0.03 per cent |
| 25,000 | 53 | 0.09 per cent |
| 50,000 | 110 | 0.33 per cent |
| 87,333 | 199 | 1.10 per cent |

**The bound on this number, stated plainly.** The corpus is a proxy. It
is this repository's prose, not a trial's conversation transcripts, and
prose written by many runs about one subject repeats its vocabulary more
than a general conversation corpus would, so the cutoff is reached
sooner here than it would be on the same number of real generation
segments. What the proxy cannot overstate is the **shape**, because the
shape follows from the arithmetic rather than from the corpus: an
absolute cutoff on a count that grows can only bite more often as the
count grows. Treat the column as the shape of the curve and not as the
value Ursa will see at a given scale.

**What the shape says, and it is the finding.** At the scale of both
trials to date the defect is exactly zero, so no trial could have found
it and the two owner-declared verdicts are untouched by it. It arrives
with growth. And the direction it fails in is the worst available one:
every span it mislabels moves from `survived_mutated`, which `CLAUDE.md`
§1 says *is* the correction expressed as an edit, into
`no_generation_provenance`, which the same section calls the most
valuable category. One error mode, silently inflating the single number
Ursa Minor sells hardest, by more as the dataset gets bigger, with no
flag on the span for a buyer or an adjudicator to filter on.

**What follows, and it is not this seat's to do.** Two things, and they
are different sizes. The small one is a flag: a span that seeded no
candidates is a different epistemic object from a span that scored below
`THETA_LOW`, and only the second one means "the matcher looked and found
nothing". Distinguishing them costs a field and no retuning. The large
one is whether `MAX_TOKEN_DF` should be relative, which is a recall and
cost tradeoff this seat has no standing to settle, because the cutoff
exists to stop the fuzzy pass from scoring every segment against every
other one. Named for the engineer, on `ursa-major/src/match.ts:12` and
`ursa-major/src/resolve.ts:399` and `:465`.

A third item is the skill agent's. The Limits section of
`skills/adjudicating-uncertain-spans/SKILL.md` calls this "a recall
problem in the matcher, not an adjudication problem," which is correct
and is now understated: the recall problem has a named cause and grows
with the corpus, and an adjudicator who knows that would sample the bare
label differently at depth than at the start.

## Finding 53: a paper this month proves that measuring human contribution from the transcript alone must miss by half in the worst case, which is an external argument for the artifact `CLAUDE.md` §1 chose and against the one it did not (high confidence on the paper's claims, verbatim abstract, and the step to Ursa is this seat's and is labelled)

**arXiv:2610.19767, *Likelihood Is Not Authorship: Can We Reliably
Measure Human Contribution in AI-Assisted Writing?*, Hasan Amin,
Zhuoran Lu and Ming Yin, submitted 2026-10-14, primary cs.CL with no
cross-list** [E152]. Read at verbatim-abstract depth.

The paper attacks the measure that estimates human contribution by
asking how predictive a person's prompt is of the final text. Verbatim
from the abstract: it introduces "SPECIFICITY PADDING that simply asks
the model to copy a few random IDs from the prompt, and that alone makes
a title-only request look more human than one that supplies the
article's key ideas." A context-swap audit then holds each document
fixed and varies only what the evaluator can see, and "the score of the
unchanged text swings by a third to half of its scale, and it rises for
every document we test, on news and scientific abstracts, across varied
LLMs from 3.8B to 753B parameters." The same articles "can average 85%
human or 15% human depending only on which prompt is on record."

The mechanism is named and it is an identity rather than an artefact:
"the score is a surprisal-weighted average over the text, so rare
strings that the context makes copyable collect the credit, whatever
they mean." And the general result, which is the part that matters here:
"If the prompt could have been machine-written, every transcript-only
method must, in the worst case, miss by half the contribution at stake.
Likelihood-based scores alone cannot reliably establish human
contribution without evidence of what the person added and how the text
was produced."

**Why this is the strongest third-party support for §1 that this seat
has found, stated carefully.** The paper's last clause names two
things a contribution measure needs, being evidence of what the person
added and evidence of how the text was produced. Ursa's artifact is
exactly those two: the span classification is what the person added,
expressed as an edit, and the backward join to every generation is how
the text was produced. The paper does not mention Ursa and makes no
claim about it, so this is this seat's inference and is labelled as
one. What the paper establishes on its own authority is narrower and
still useful: the class of method Ursa did not choose has a proven worst
case, and `CLAUDE.md` §1's assertion that the label must be "supplied by
the artifact rather than by a grader" now has an independent argument
under it instead of only an assertion.

**And the bound that keeps this honest.** Ursa's classifier is not
likelihood-based, so the paper's attack does not reach it. This run
checked rather than assumed: `ursa-major/src/match.ts` opens by saying
the design is "deliberately lexical and deterministic — no embeddings,
no model judging similarity," and `containment` weights every token
equally rather than by surprisal [E157]. So the credit-ledger identity
has no analogue in the scoring. It does have one in the **seeding**,
which is finding 52, and the two findings should not be merged: the
paper's problem is that rare strings collect too much credit, and Ursa's
is that common strings get no look at all. Opposite ends of the same
axis, reached by different mechanisms.

**The reusable part, for whoever builds the next measurement here.** The
context-swap audit is a method, not a result. Hold the artifact fixed,
vary only what the measuring instrument can see, and report the swing.
That is the design finding 52's measurement used without knowing the
paper names it, and it is the cheapest available test of any future
change to the matcher.

## Finding 54: finding 14's defect now has a published vocabulary, a measured gap, and a reason the honest fix is permanent disclosure rather than a backlog item (high confidence, full text)

**arXiv:2610.15991, *Revocation Is Not Complete at Receipt: An
Executable Outcome Contract for Distributed Agent-Tool Gateways*, Lifei
Liu, Haoran Yu and Danping Zhang, submitted 2026-10-11, primary cs.AI
cross-listed cs.CR** [E151]. Read at full-text depth. This was lead 2 of
the eight the fourteenth run left at title depth, and it is the one that
paid.

The paper defines a six-stage contract, verbatim: "C0 Issuer committed.
The authoritative issuer records the revocation. C1 Queued... C2
Transport acknowledged... C3 Persisted. A receiver verifies and durably
stores the event. C4 Gateway enforced. Every assigned gateway durably
installs deny state and emits a receipt binding the gateway, delegation,
event, channel, and application time. C5 Effect prevented. For every
assigned gateway g, no guarded effect attributable to g commits in the
declared post-revocation observation window."

Measured, under selective receiver-to-gateway non-delivery: "receiver B
still records HTTP 202 ingress and durable PERSISTED state in every
block, but records no gateway receipt. The treatment admits 3,360 calls,
and every admitted call commits: 1,680 files and 1,680 PostgreSQL rows."
The paired treatment effect is "exactly +49.78 percentage points for
both stale admission and committed effects in every block of both
executions." And the conclusion: "Transport success is therefore
evidence of progress, not a revocation-completion verdict."

**What this adds to finding 14, which is already filed and is not
refiled.** Finding 14, in `docs/research/briefs/2026-10-24.md`,
established that Ursa Major's revocation has three channels, that the
local store is enforced, that the aggregate batch is out of reach and
says so in `docs/design/consent-and-erasure.md` §10.2, and that the
paste channel is out of reach and says so nowhere. This run confirmed
the defect is still open: `ursa-major/src/consent.cli.ts:154` still
prints the unqualified sentence, and §10's list still has five items
and none of them is the paste channel [E160]. A search of all pull
requests in every state and a grep of the committed files found no fix
and no disclosure text anywhere, open or merged [E161].

Three things are new, and they change the shape of the fix rather than
restating the defect.

**One, the channels now map onto a published ladder, which makes the
overclaim precise instead of rhetorical.** The local store reaches C4
and C5. The aggregate batch reaches C0 and discloses that it stops
there. The paste channel reaches C0 only. And the sentence the CLI
prints at the moment of revocation, "it is excluded from every export
and from the overlay," is a C5 claim. The product asserts effect
prevention on a channel that has not left issuer commit.

**Two, the gap between C3 and C4 is now measured by someone else, at
49.78 points.** That number is not Ursa's and does not transfer to
Ursa's architecture. What transfers is that the distance between
"recorded" and "effective" is the normal case rather than an edge case,
large, and invisible from the issuer's side. This is the first external
measurement this seat has found of the thing finding 14 argued from
first principles.

**Three, and this is the part that should change what the engineer
does.** C4 requires that every assigned gateway "emits a receipt." The
paste channel's gateway is a text field inside another vendor's product.
It cannot emit a receipt, it cannot be enumerated, and the paper's own
monitor returns bounded completion "only after every expected gateway
has a receipt." So the paste channel is not an unbuilt C4. It is
**structurally incapable** of C4, permanently, for the same reason that
makes it the product's most valuable delivery route: it works without
asking the vendor for anything.

That converts finding 14's recommendation. It is not "the sentence needs
a clause, someday". It is that the paste channel belongs in §10, the
list of things this system does not do, next to §10.2's existing
disclosure for the aggregate batch, written in the same voice, as a
permanent property rather than a deferred item. `CLAUDE.md`'s second
load-bearing constraint is that the user can always see, edit, revoke
and delete, with no dark patterns, and the dark pattern available here
is not a lie. It is a true sentence printed at the one moment a user
cannot check it.

## Finding 55: the closest published formalism to Ursa Major's own claim treats the user's sharing preferences as given and never asks who wrote them, which is the half Ursa actually has (high confidence, full text, absence claims licensed by it)

**arXiv:2610.27213, *Profile-DP: Differential Privacy for
User-Controlled Profile Release in Personalized LLM Inference*, Mingchen
Li, Zifan Peng, Kewei Sha, Lavinia F Pieptea, Heng Fan, Junhua Ding,
Meikang Qiu and Yunhe Feng, submitted 2026-10-19, primary cs.CR
cross-listed cs.AI and cs.CL** [E150]. Read at full-text depth. This was
lead 1 of the fourteenth run's eight, ranked first for this run by that
run, and it is filed fourth here because what it actually contains is
narrower than its title promised.

The paper gives a formal guarantee for releasing a user profile to a
model: "For each fixed public context c, a local profile-release
mechanism M satisfies ε-Profile-DP if, for every u ≃c u′ and output
event S, Pr[M(u;c) ∈ S] ≤ e^ε Pr[M(u′;c) ∈ S]." It treats "the public
instruction q [and] the profile schema" plus every shared fact as public
side information, and bounds the release of the facts the user withheld.
Two mechanisms, DP-SLOT for independent release and DP-BLOCK for joint
release. Evaluated on "a fixed random sample of 500 PEEP records"
derived from WildChat with synthetic sharing preferences.

**What it is good for here.** It is the first published formalism this
seat has found that takes Ursa Major's object as its object: a profile
the user governs, released selectively to a model, with the withheld
part provably bounded. If Ursa Major ever has to argue that selective
release is a formal property rather than a product promise, this is the
citation and `ε` is the knob.

**And the absence, which the full text licenses and the abstract page
would not have.** Asked for the section list and then for named
contents, rather than asked whether a topic appears, the paper has no
treatment of revocation, consent withdrawal, deletion, or cross-vendor
portability anywhere [E150]. It also never says who authors the sharing
preferences or how they are enforced: PEEP's preferences are synthetic
and per-attribute, and authorship and enforcement are simply not in the
document.

That absence is the finding. The hard part of Ursa Major is not bounding
the release of a withheld fact, which this paper solves with a budget.
It is that a real user's preferences are authored by the user, change,
and are revoked, and that the release has already happened by the time
she changes her mind. Profile-DP assumes the policy and solves the
release. Ursa has the policy problem and, by finding 54, an unenforced
release path. **The literature is building the half Ursa does not need
yet and is silent on the half that is load-bearing**, which is a
better-evidenced version of the 2026-10-09 brief's reading and is worth
carrying into any conversation with a lab about Major's architecture.
